Dynamics and measured values

1.0 assumes about 0.004" (0.1 mm) of flank contact. Worn or honed edges damp more. Use the calibrate button with a cut you know runs clean to set this from your own shop.

Typical damping: 0.02–0.05 for an end mill in a holder, 0.01–0.03 for a solid boring bar. A tap test (impact hammer and accelerometer at the tool tip) gives the real frequency, stiffness and damping and makes the lobes far more reliable.

How it works

  • Tool model. The tool is a stepped carbide or steel cantilever: fluted section at an effective diameter of about 0.75–0.83 D (by flute count), optional neck, then shank. Clamp give is added as extra effective stickout (0.25 D for shrink fit up to 0.6 D for a set screw), and the spindle is a spring in series. The first bending mode comes from a Rayleigh estimate on the static deflection shape.
  • Chatter model. Regenerative chatter on one symmetric mode. Milling uses the averaged (zero-order) directional-factor method of Altintas and Budak; boring uses the classic single-point turning limit with the radial force ratio Kr. Unequal flute spacing is handled with a separate delay for each flute. Helix angle and runout are not modeled.
  • Process damping. At low surface speed the chatter waves on the part get very short, and the flank of the edge rubs on them and absorbs vibration. This is why titanium and Inconel can be cut deep at low SFM. It is modeled as damping that grows with depth and falls with surface speed (Chiou–Liang indentation model). Its strength depends on edge prep and wear, so calibrating it with one known-good cut is the best way to set it.
  • Scaling rule. Cutting force is cut by the stiffness ratio (long tool ÷ catalog-length tool) so tip deflection stays at what the catalog tool would see. Most of that cut comes from depth; chip load drops only by the ratio to the 0.25 power so the edge keeps cutting instead of rubbing. Depth is then capped at the safety factor times the stability limit at the chosen speed, and chip load is raised back if depth was the tighter limit.
  • Accuracy. Stiffness and frequency are usually within 10–25% of reality; lobe positions shift with any frequency error, so the pocket speeds are a starting point. Cut a test pass at the scaled values, listen, and nudge rpm ±5% to find the quiet spot.
